I have taught economics since 2006, in Turkey, the United States and Australia. Almost all of this teaching has been in the technical core of the degree: mathematics for economists, microeconomic theory and econometrics.

In a technical course the hard part for a student is rarely the final answer. It is knowing how to begin, and what to do when the first attempt goes nowhere. So I solve problems by hand, in real time, writing on a tablet that is projected to the room.

Keeping a class engaged

I was hooked on game theory as an undergraduate at Koç University because Levent Koçkesen had us play the games before he analysed them. I have taught with that in mind ever since, and I get students involved through activities of this kind as often as I can. One example is a guessing game, played at the start of a semester, in which the winner is whoever comes closest to one-third of the class average. Students answer on their phones through an online poll, and I have an AI model analyse the guesses in front of them, so that the theory arrives as an explanation of what the room has just done. Another is a lightning-round quiz I built for the mathematics courses: about fifty quick questions, answered by scanning a QR code that opens a website I wrote for the purpose.

Teaching tools

With Axel Wieneke, a course coordinator at Queensland, I built Class Groups in 2026, a web application for forming teams and booking presentation times in large courses. The students serve themselves: any member of a team can put forward a ranked list of presentation times or support a list a teammate has proposed, and when the team is unanimous the time is allocated at once. It was used for a course of about 200 students and greatly reduced the coordination work.
